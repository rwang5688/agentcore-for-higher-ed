## Course Description

### Course Description

COMP-16900: The Beauty and Joy of Computing offers an advanced exploration of computational aesthetics, human-centered design principles, and the philosophical dimensions of computer science as both a discipline and a cultural force. Students will engage with foundational and cutting-edge topics in computing through a lens that emphasizes creativity, ethical responsibility, and the transformative potential of technology in society. The course draws on interdisciplinary perspectives to challenge graduate-level students to think critically about how computing shapes—and is shaped by—human experience.

---

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

- Critically analyze the aesthetic and ethical dimensions of computational systems, articulating how design choices reflect and reinforce cultural values
- Synthesize advanced theoretical frameworks from computer science, philosophy of technology, and human-computer interaction to evaluate emerging computational paradigms
- Design and evaluate computing artifacts that balance technical rigor with principles of accessibility, inclusivity, and social responsibility
- Demonstrate a nuanced understanding of the historical trajectory of computing, connecting landmark innovations to broader intellectual and humanistic traditions
- Communicate complex computational ideas effectively to both technical and non-technical audiences through written, oral, and demonstrative formats

### Prerequisites

Given the 700-level standing of this course, students are expected to have substantial prior preparation in computing and related disciplines. The following prerequisites are required:

- Completion of COMP-50200: Foundations of Computer Science Theory or equivalent
- COMP-61100: Human-Computer Interaction or COMP-61400: Computing Ethics and Society
- Graduate standing in the College of Engineering or permission of the instructor
- Demonstrated proficiency in at least one high-level programming language, as evidenced by prior coursework or portfolio submission

Students lacking formal prerequisites but possessing equivalent professional experience may petition the department for a prerequisite waiver no later than two weeks before the semester begins.

### Assessment Methods

Student performance will be evaluated through a combination of written, practical, and collaborative assessments designed to reflect the interdisciplinary nature of the course:

- **Seminar Participation and Weekly Reflections (15%):** Students will contribute to structured in-class discussions and submit brief weekly reflection papers (300–500 words) responding to assigned readings and guest lectures. Reflections will be evaluated for depth of engagement and critical thinking.

- **Analytical Essay (20%):** A mid-semester essay (2,500–3,500 words) in which students critically examine a particular computational system, programming language, or technological movement through the aesthetic and ethical frameworks introduced in the course.

- **Computational Artifact Project (30%):** A substantive individual or paired project in which students design, implement, and document a computing artifact that embodies one or more of the course's guiding principles. Project deliverables include a working prototype, technical documentation, and a reflective design rationale.

- **Research Symposium Presentation (15%):** Near the end of the semester, students will present their project work in a formal symposium format, fielding questions from peers and faculty panel members. Presentations will be evaluated on clarity, depth, and scholarly rigor.

- **Final Capstone Paper (20%):** A graduate-level research paper (6,000–8,000 words) connecting the student's project work to broader scholarly literature on computing, society, and aesthetics. Papers must conform to ACM or IEEE citation standards and are due during the final examination period.
