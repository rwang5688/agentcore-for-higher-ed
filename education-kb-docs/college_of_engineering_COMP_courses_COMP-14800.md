## Course Description

### Course Description

Programming Languages Part A provides an in-depth examination of the theoretical foundations and practical paradigms underlying modern programming languages, with emphasis on syntax, semantics, and language design principles. Students explore a broad spectrum of programming paradigms—including functional, object-oriented, logic-based, and imperative approaches—developing the analytical skills necessary to evaluate and compare language constructs critically. This course serves as the first installment of a two-part sequence, equipping students with the conceptual vocabulary and technical grounding required for advanced study in language theory, compiler design, and software engineering.

### Learning Objectives

Upon successful completion of this course, students will be able to:

- Identify and differentiate among major programming paradigms, articulating the strengths, limitations, and appropriate use cases of each
- Analyze the formal syntax of programming languages using grammars, parse trees, and related notation systems such as BNF and EBNF
- Apply foundational concepts of static and dynamic semantics to evaluate program correctness and predict runtime behavior
- Compare and contrast type systems, scoping rules, and binding mechanisms across a representative selection of contemporary and historical languages
- Demonstrate practical proficiency in at least two paradigmatically distinct programming languages by implementing well-structured solutions to substantive computational problems

### Assessment Methods

| Component | Weight |
|---|---|
| Midterm Examination | 20% |
| Final Examination | 25% |
| Programming Assignments (4–5 over the semester) | 30% |
| Language Analysis Paper | 15% |
| Participation and In-Class Exercises | 10% |

**Midterm and Final Examinations** assess students' theoretical understanding of language paradigms, formal grammar, type systems, and semantics. Both exams include a combination of short-answer, multiple-choice, and problem-solving questions.

**Programming Assignments** require students to implement solutions in languages representing distinct paradigms (e.g., Haskell, Prolog, Python, C). Assignments are evaluated on correctness, clarity, and adherence to idiomatic language conventions.

**Language Analysis Paper** (approximately 2,500–3,500 words) tasks students with conducting a structured comparative analysis of two programming languages of their choice, drawing on course concepts to evaluate design decisions, trade-offs, and suitability for defined application domains. A brief proposal and one round of instructor feedback are built into the timeline.

**Participation and In-Class Exercises** reflect consistent, substantive engagement with discussions and short in-class coding or analysis activities conducted throughout the semester.
