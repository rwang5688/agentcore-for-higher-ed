## Course Description

### Course Description

COMP-17000: The Bits and Bytes of Computer Networking is an advanced graduate-level course that provides a rigorous examination of the theoretical foundations and practical implementations of modern computer networking systems. Students will explore network architecture, protocol design, data transmission principles, and emerging network technologies within both enterprise and distributed computing environments. This course emphasizes both analytical problem-solving and hands-on configuration, equipping students with the expertise necessary to design, manage, and troubleshoot complex networking infrastructures.

---

### Learning Objectives

Upon successful completion of this course, students will be able to:

- Analyze and evaluate the OSI and TCP/IP reference models, demonstrating a comprehensive understanding of how each layer contributes to end-to-end network communication
- Design and implement scalable network topologies, selecting appropriate protocols, addressing schemes, and routing strategies to meet specific performance and security requirements
- Critically assess network security vulnerabilities and apply advanced mitigation strategies, including firewall configuration, intrusion detection, and encrypted tunneling protocols
- Evaluate the performance of network systems using simulation tools and packet analysis software, and propose evidence-based optimizations to improve throughput, latency, and reliability
- Synthesize current research literature to assess the impact of emerging technologies — including software-defined networking (SDN), network function virtualization (NFV), and next-generation wireless standards — on the evolution of network design

### Prerequisites

Students enrolling in COMP-17000 must have completed the following or demonstrate equivalent graduate-level competency:

- **COMP-72400: Advanced Data Communications and Network Fundamentals** or equivalent
- **COMP-68150: Operating Systems Architecture and Systems Programming**
- **COMP-71300: Algorithms and Computational Complexity**
- Demonstrated proficiency in at least one systems-level programming language (C, C++, or Python)
- Admission to a graduate program within the College of Engineering, or explicit written permission from the supervising instructor

Students lacking formal prerequisites may petition the Department of Computer Science for a prerequisite waiver, subject to review of prior coursework and professional experience.

### Assessment Methods

Student performance in COMP-17000 will be evaluated through the following components:

| Assessment Component | Weight |
|---|---|
| Midterm Examination | 20% |
| Final Examination | 25% |
| Research Paper | 20% |
| Lab Projects & Practicals (×4) | 25% |
| Seminar Participation & Presentations | 10% |

**Midterm and Final Examinations:** Both examinations are closed-book and will assess students' mastery of theoretical concepts, protocol analysis, and network design problem-solving. The final examination is cumulative and includes a practical network configuration component administered in the departmental networking laboratory.

**Research Paper:** Students will produce an original 6,000–8,000 word research paper investigating a current or emerging topic in computer networking. The paper must adhere to IEEE formatting standards, engage substantively with peer-reviewed literature, and include an original analysis or proposed framework. A topic proposal and annotated bibliography are due at mid-semester; the final paper is submitted during the final week of instruction.

**Lab Projects & Practicals:** Four structured laboratory projects will be conducted throughout the semester, using industry-standard simulation and packet analysis tools. Projects will include tasks such as VLAN configuration, routing protocol implementation, firewall rule development, and network performance benchmarking. Each project requires a written technical report.

**Seminar Participation & Presentations:** Students are expected to engage actively in weekly seminar discussions. Each student will deliver one formal 20-minute presentation on an assigned topic drawn from current networking research, followed by a structured class Q&A session.
