### Course Description

This course provides a rigorous and comprehensive introduction to the design, analysis, and implementation of fundamental algorithms and data structures, with emphasis on efficiency, correctness, and scalability. Students will explore classical algorithmic paradigms including divide-and-conquer, dynamic programming, greedy strategies, and graph-based methods, developing both theoretical understanding and practical problem-solving skills. The course bridges foundational computational theory with real-world software engineering challenges encountered in advanced academic and professional contexts.

---

### Learning Objectives

Upon successful completion of this course, students will be able to:

- Analyze the time and space complexity of algorithms using asymptotic notation, including Big-O, Big-Omega, and Big-Theta frameworks, and apply recurrence relations to evaluate recursive algorithms.
- Design and implement efficient algorithmic solutions by selecting and applying appropriate paradigms — including divide-and-conquer, dynamic programming, and greedy methods — to a broad range of computational problems.
- Demonstrate proficiency with core data structures such as heaps, balanced binary search trees, hash tables, and graphs, and reason about the trade-offs involved in their selection and use.
- Construct formal proofs of algorithm correctness using techniques such as loop invariants, induction, and reduction, and communicate these arguments clearly in written and oral form.
- Evaluate and compare algorithmic approaches to NP-completeness, approximation algorithms, and computational intractability, situating these concepts within the broader landscape of theoretical computer science.

### Assessment Methods

| Component | Weight |
|---|---|
| Problem Sets (6 assignments) | 30% |
| Midterm Examination | 20% |
| Algorithm Design Project | 25% |
| Final Examination | 20% |
| Participation & In-Class Exercises | 5% |

**Problem Sets:** Six written and programming assignments distributed throughout the semester, requiring students to analyze, design, and implement algorithms solving progressively complex problems. Collaboration is permitted in discussion only; all submitted work must be independently authored.

**Midterm Examination:** A closed-book, in-person examination covering algorithmic analysis, sorting and searching, divide-and-conquer strategies, and foundational data structures. Students may bring one double-sided handwritten reference sheet.

**Algorithm Design Project:** A substantial individual or paired project in which students identify a real-world computational problem, survey relevant algorithmic literature, design an original or adapted solution, implement it in a language of their choice, and submit both a working codebase and a formal written report of approximately 3,000–4,500 words. An oral defense component may be required at instructor discretion.

**Final Examination:** A comprehensive closed-book examination covering all course material, with particular emphasis on dynamic programming, graph algorithms, and computational complexity theory. One double-sided handwritten reference sheet is permitted.
