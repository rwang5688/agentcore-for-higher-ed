## Course Description

### Course Description

COMP-2800 equips students with the critical analytical tools necessary to identify, evaluate, and challenge misleading claims, flawed methodologies, and manipulative uses of data in an era increasingly driven by big data and algorithmic decision-making. Drawing from computer science, statistics, and information literacy, the course examines how data can be selectively presented, misrepresented, or weaponized to mislead public discourse, policy, and research. Students will develop a rigorous, evidence-based framework for assessing quantitative claims encountered in media, academic literature, corporate communications, and political messaging.

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

- Identify common statistical fallacies, sampling errors, and methodological weaknesses in data-driven arguments presented across a range of media and professional contexts
- Apply foundational principles of data literacy and computational reasoning to critically evaluate claims made using large datasets, machine learning outputs, and algorithmic systems
- Distinguish between correlation and causation, and recognize when causal language is being used inappropriately or deceptively in quantitative reporting
- Construct and communicate well-reasoned counter-arguments to misleading data claims, using appropriate statistical evidence and logical reasoning
- Evaluate the ethical responsibilities of data scientists, journalists, and institutions in the accurate and transparent communication of quantitative findings

### Prerequisites

Students must have successfully completed the following prior to enrollment:

- **COMP-1100: Introduction to Computer Science** or equivalent foundational programming course
- **MATH-1210: Introduction to Statistics and Probability** or **MATH-1200: Discrete Mathematics**
- Completion of at least 30 credit points at the undergraduate level is strongly recommended

Students without the statistical prerequisite may seek instructor permission; however, comfort with basic probability, descriptive statistics, and data interpretation is assumed throughout the course.

### Assessment Methods

| Assessment Component | Weight | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly Response Papers | 20% | Short written responses (300–500 words) critiquing a real-world data claim drawn from current events, submitted before each class session |
| Midterm Examination | 20% | In-class written examination testing comprehension of statistical reasoning, logical fallacies, and course concepts covered in Weeks 1–7 |
| Data Audit Project | 25% | Individual or paired project in which students select a published dataset or data-driven news story and produce a structured audit report identifying methodological concerns and misleading claims |
| Final Debate and Presentation | 20% | Students are assigned a contested data-driven claim and must argue a position, respond to peer challenges, and demonstrate real-time critical reasoning skills |
| Final Examination | 15% | Comprehensive take-home examination requiring extended critical analysis of a novel dataset or statistical argument not previously encountered in coursework |
| Participation and Engagement | 10% | Active, informed participation in seminar discussions, peer critique exercises, and in-class workshops |
