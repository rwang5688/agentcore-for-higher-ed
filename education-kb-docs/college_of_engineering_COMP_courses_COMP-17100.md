## Course Description

### Course Description

COMP-17100 examines the emerging technologies, architectures, and sociotechnical forces shaping the next generation of the Internet, with particular attention to decentralization, artificial intelligence integration, and evolving network infrastructure. Students will critically analyze current trajectories in Internet development, including Web3 paradigms, edge computing, semantic web initiatives, and the geopolitics of digital connectivity. This course bridges theoretical frameworks with practical foresight methodologies to equip students with the analytical tools necessary to anticipate and influence the Internet's future landscape.

---

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

- Evaluate competing visions for next-generation Internet architectures, including decentralized, federated, and AI-augmented network models, and assess their technical feasibility and societal implications
- Apply structured foresight and technology forecasting methodologies to construct evidence-based scenarios for the evolution of Internet infrastructure and governance
- Critically assess the ethical, political, and economic dimensions of emerging Internet technologies, including issues of digital sovereignty, surveillance, algorithmic control, and equitable access
- Synthesize current research literature in distributed systems, human-computer interaction, and network science to formulate original arguments about the Internet's developmental trajectory
- Design and present a rigorous forward-looking research proposal addressing a substantive open problem in Internet technology, policy, or design

---

### Prerequisites

Given the advanced graduate level of this course (965), students are expected to have substantial prior preparation in relevant areas. The following prerequisites apply:

- Completion of a graduate-level course in computer networks or distributed systems (equivalent to COMP-72400 or above)
- Demonstrated proficiency in research methods and academic writing at the graduate level
- A foundational understanding of Internet protocols, web technologies, and network architecture equivalent to undergraduate coursework in computer science or a closely related discipline
- Graduate standing within the College of Engineering or written permission of the supervising instructor

Students from outside the Department of Computer Science are encouraged to consult with one of the course instructors prior to enrollment to confirm readiness.

### Assessment Methods

| Component | Weight | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Seminar Participation | 15% | Active, substantive engagement in weekly seminar discussions, including peer critique and response to assigned readings |
| Critical Response Papers | 20% | Four short response papers (500–750 words each) analyzing assigned readings and situating them within broader course themes |
| Technology Foresight Analysis | 25% | A midterm analytical report (4,000–5,000 words) applying a foresight methodology of the student's choice to a specified emerging Internet technology |
| Final Research Paper | 30% | An original, independently researched paper (8,000–10,000 words) presenting a novel analysis of a chosen aspect of the Internet's future trajectory, subject to peer review within the cohort |
| Presentation and Defense | 10% | A 20-minute formal presentation of the final research paper, followed by a structured question-and-answer session with the seminar panel |

Late submissions will be penalized at a rate of 5% per calendar day unless an extension has been approved in writing by a course instructor prior to the deadline.

### Required Materials

All required texts are available through the Peculiar U library system or the course's digital learning portal. Students are expected to have completed the assigned readings prior to each seminar session.

Students are also strongly encouraged — though not required — to maintain access to a current edition of an Internet standards reference, such as the IETF RFC repository, for use in technical components of their research.
