### Course Description

This course traces the evolving relationship between human societies and the natural environment across North American history, from Indigenous land stewardship through the industrial era to contemporary environmental policy debates. Students will examine how economic development, cultural values, and political institutions have shaped — and been shaped by — the landscapes, ecosystems, and natural resources of the American continent. Special attention is given to questions of environmental justice, the legacy of colonialism, and the role of science and engineering in both environmental degradation and ecological restoration.

---

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

- Identify and analyze major turning points in American environmental history, including key legislative milestones, ecological crises, and shifts in public attitudes toward nature and conservation
- Critically evaluate the ways in which race, class, gender, and Indigenous sovereignty have influenced access to natural resources and exposure to environmental hazards throughout American history
- Apply foundational concepts from environmental science and ecology to interpret historical land use patterns, resource extraction practices, and their long-term ecological consequences
- Construct evidence-based historical arguments using primary sources such as government documents, naturalist writings, policy records, and oral histories from affected communities
- Connect historical environmental trends to present-day challenges in sustainability, resource management, and environmental engineering, demonstrating an understanding of how the past informs contemporary practice

### Prerequisites

Students enrolling in ENVI-0200 should have completed at least one of the following:

- **ENVI-0101: Introduction to Environmental Science** or equivalent
- **HIST-0110: Survey of American History** or equivalent introductory history course
- Instructor consent for students with relevant interdisciplinary background

A minimum of 30 completed credit hours or sophomore standing is strongly recommended. No prior technical engineering coursework is required, though familiarity with basic ecological concepts will be advantageous.

### Assessment Methods

| Component | Weight |
|---|---|
| Midterm Examination | 20% |
| Final Examination | 20% |
| Primary Source Analysis Papers (×2) | 25% |
| Research Paper (2,500–3,000 words) | 25% |
| Participation and Weekly Reading Responses | 10% |

**Midterm and Final Examinations:** Both examinations consist of short-answer and essay questions designed to assess students' command of course chronology, key themes, and analytical frameworks. The final examination is cumulative.

**Primary Source Analysis Papers:** Students will submit two short analytical papers (800–1,000 words each) engaging closely with assigned primary documents. Papers must situate the source within its historical context and address its implications for environmental and social history.

**Research Paper:** A substantive research paper requiring students to develop an original argument about a specific episode, movement, or policy in American environmental history. Topics must be approved by the instructor no later than Week 6. The paper must draw on a minimum of eight scholarly sources and demonstrate integration of both historical and environmental perspectives.

**Participation and Weekly Reading Responses:** Students are expected to attend all sessions and contribute meaningfully to discussion. Brief written responses (150–200 words) to assigned readings are due before class on designated weeks and will be assessed for engagement and critical reflection.
