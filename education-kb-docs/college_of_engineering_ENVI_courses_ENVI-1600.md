## Course Description

### Course Description

This advanced seminar examines the long-term dynamics of Earth's carbon cycle and climate system, drawing on the foundational research and pedagogical frameworks developed by David Archer of the University of Chicago. Students will engage critically with the physical, chemical, and biological processes that govern atmospheric CO₂ concentrations across geological and human timescales, with particular emphasis on the feedbacks between ocean chemistry, terrestrial carbon reservoirs, and global temperature. The course bridges theoretical modelling approaches with real-world observational data to develop sophisticated, quantitative understandings of anthropogenic climate forcing and its projected long-term consequences.

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

- Critically evaluate the principal mechanisms driving carbon exchange between the atmosphere, oceans, and terrestrial biosphere, and articulate how disruptions to these fluxes propagate through Earth's climate system over centennial to millennial timescales
- Apply and interpret numerical and conceptual models of the global carbon cycle to generate and assess projections of future atmospheric greenhouse gas concentrations under a range of emissions scenarios
- Synthesise primary scientific literature and quantitative datasets to construct evidence-based arguments regarding climate sensitivity, ocean acidification, and the long-term fate of anthropogenic carbon emissions
- Analyse the sociotechnical dimensions of carbon management strategies — including carbon capture, sequestration, and natural sink enhancement — evaluating their feasibility, scalability, and limitations within Earth system constraints
- Communicate complex geophysical and biogeochemical findings to both specialist and non-specialist audiences through written, visual, and oral modalities appropriate to professional engineering and environmental science contexts

### Assessment Methods

**Individual Assignments (20%)**
Three written problem sets distributed across the term, requiring quantitative analysis of carbon cycle data, box-model outputs, and climate sensitivity calculations. Each problem set is designed to reinforce lecture content and develop facility with the computational tools introduced in class.

**Critical Literature Review (15%)**
A structured review essay of approximately 2,500 words in which students evaluate a corpus of peer-reviewed literature on an assigned topic within the carbon-climate system. Assessment emphasises analytical depth, engagement with methodological critique, and quality of scientific argumentation.

**Group Modelling Project (25%)**
Conducted in teams of three to four, this project requires students to design, run, and interpret a computational simulation of a specified carbon cycle perturbation scenario. Teams will submit a written technical report (4,000–5,000 words) and deliver a 20-minute oral presentation with Q&A to a panel of course instructors.

**Midterm Examination (15%)**
A two-hour in-class examination covering foundational concepts from the first half of the course, including carbon cycle mechanics, ocean carbonate chemistry, and greenhouse forcing principles. Both short-answer and extended analytical response formats are included.

**Final Examination (25%)**
A three-hour comprehensive examination administered during the formal examination period. Students will be expected to integrate material from across the full course, demonstrating mastery of both theoretical frameworks and their applied implications for climate policy and engineering practice.
