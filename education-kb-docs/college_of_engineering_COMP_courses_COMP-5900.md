## Course Description

### Course Description

COMP-5900 provides an in-depth examination of the design, analysis, and implementation of fundamental and advanced algorithms, with emphasis on correctness proofs, computational complexity, and the application of algorithmic paradigms to complex problem domains. Students will explore classical and contemporary algorithmic techniques including divide-and-conquer, dynamic programming, greedy methods, graph algorithms, and approximation strategies for NP-hard problems. This course bridges theoretical foundations with practical problem-solving, preparing students to evaluate algorithmic trade-offs and engineer efficient computational solutions in professional and research contexts.

---

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

- Analyze the time and space complexity of algorithms using asymptotic notation, recurrence relations, and formal proof techniques, including induction and loop invariants.
- Design efficient algorithms by applying paradigms such as divide-and-conquer, dynamic programming, greedy strategies, and randomization to novel and well-defined computational problems.
- Evaluate the tractability of computational problems by classifying them within complexity classes, including P, NP, NP-complete, and NP-hard, and construct formal reductions between problems.
- Implement and empirically validate algorithmic solutions, comparing theoretical predictions of performance against observed behavior across varied input conditions.
- Develop and apply approximation algorithms and heuristic methods for optimization problems where exact polynomial-time solutions are not known to exist.

### Prerequisites

Students must have successfully completed the following before enrolling in COMP-5900:

- **COMP-3210: Data Structures and Algorithm Fundamentals** — foundational understanding of common data structures (trees, graphs, heaps, hash tables) and introductory algorithm analysis is assumed throughout this course.
- **COMP-3450: Discrete Mathematics for Computer Science** — proficiency in logic, set theory, combinatorics, and mathematical proof is essential for the rigorous analysis undertaken in this course.
- **COMP-4120: Theory of Computation** — prior exposure to formal languages, automata, and introductory complexity theory is required to engage meaningfully with the NP-completeness material.
- **MATH-2310: Linear Algebra** — recommended but not strictly required; students without this background should consult the course instructors prior to enrollment.

Students who have not satisfied the above prerequisites must obtain written departmental approval before registering.

### Assessment Methods

Student performance in COMP-5900 will be evaluated through the following components:

| Component | Weight |
|---|---|
| Problem Sets (6 assignments) | 30% |
| Midterm Examination | 20% |
| Algorithm Design Project | 25% |
| Final Examination | 20% |
| Participation and In-Class Problem Solving | 5% |

**Problem Sets:** Six written assignments distributed throughout the semester, each requiring students to design, analyze, and prove the correctness of algorithms. Collaboration is permitted within documented boundaries as outlined in the Academic Integrity Policy; submitted work must be independently written.

**Midterm Examination:** A closed-book, in-person examination covering all material from the first half of the course. Students are permitted one double-sided handwritten reference sheet.

**Algorithm Design Project:** A substantial individual or paired research project in which students identify a problem domain, survey relevant algorithmic literature, design or adapt an algorithm, and present a formal analysis of correctness and complexity. A written report and a brief oral presentation to the class are both required deliverables.

**Final Examination:** A comprehensive closed-book examination administered during the university's official final examination period. One double-sided handwritten reference sheet is permitted.

**Participation:** Students are expected to engage actively during lecture discussions and collaborative problem-solving sessions. Quality of contribution is weighted over frequency.
