## Course Description

### Course Description

COMP-8000: Innovative Computing is an advanced graduate-level seminar that explores the cutting edge of computational theory, design, and application, with a focus on emerging paradigms that challenge conventional approaches to problem-solving in computer science. Students will critically examine transformative technologies including quantum computing architectures, neuromorphic systems, edge intelligence, and human-computer interaction models that are reshaping the boundaries of the discipline. Through rigorous research, collaborative inquiry, and hands-on experimentation, students develop the capacity to conceptualize, evaluate, and contribute original ideas to the rapidly evolving landscape of modern computing.

### Learning Objectives

Upon successful completion of this course, students will be able to:

- Critically analyze and synthesize current and emerging trends in innovative computing, situating them within broader theoretical and practical frameworks of computer science
- Design and propose novel computational solutions to complex, open-ended problems by applying advanced principles drawn from quantum, neuromorphic, distributed, and intelligent systems paradigms
- Evaluate the ethical, societal, and technical implications of disruptive computing technologies, articulating informed positions supported by scholarly evidence
- Conduct independent research at the graduate level, producing work of sufficient rigor and originality to contribute meaningfully to academic or professional discourse in the field
- Collaborate effectively in interdisciplinary research teams, demonstrating leadership in ideation, prototyping, and critical peer review of innovative computational projects

### Prerequisites

Given the advanced graduate standing required for COMP-8000 (Level 6835), students must have satisfied the following prerequisites prior to enrollment:

- COMP-6200: Advanced Algorithms and Complexity Theory or equivalent graduate-level coursework
- COMP-6450: Graduate Seminar in Machine Learning Systems or instructor approval
- COMP-6310: Distributed Systems Architecture
- Demonstrated proficiency in at least two high-level or systems-level programming languages (e.g., Python, C++, Julia, or Rust), verified through prior coursework or portfolio submission
- Graduate standing in the Department of Computer Science or written permission of the supervising instructor

Students from outside the Department of Computer Science seeking enrollment must obtain approval from both their home department and the course instructor.

### Assessment Methods

Assessment in COMP-8000 is designed to reflect the research-intensive and innovation-driven nature of the course. Students are evaluated through the following components:

- **Research Literature Review (15%):** A structured review of no fewer than fifteen peer-reviewed sources on a self-selected emerging computing topic, submitted at the end of Week 4. The review must demonstrate critical synthesis rather than simple summarization.
- **Seminar Participation and Facilitation (15%):** Active and substantive contribution to weekly seminar discussions, including one session in which the student serves as lead discussant, responsible for framing the conversation, presenting key arguments, and moderating peer dialogue.
- **Midterm Research Proposal (20%):** A formal written proposal (approximately 3,000–4,500 words) outlining an original research question, theoretical grounding, proposed methodology, and anticipated contributions to the field of innovative computing.
- **Prototype or Proof-of-Concept Implementation (25%):** A working technical demonstration or computational prototype aligned with the student's research focus, accompanied by documented code, architectural diagrams, and a written technical report justifying design decisions.
- **Final Research Paper and Presentation (25%):** A publication-quality research paper (7,000–10,000 words) submitted at the end of the semester, along with a formal 25-minute conference-style oral presentation delivered to faculty and peers during the final examination period. Papers of exceptional merit may be nominated by the instructors for submission to relevant academic conferences or journals.

No traditional closed-book examinations are administered in this course, reflecting its emphasis on sustained intellectual inquiry over rote assessment.
