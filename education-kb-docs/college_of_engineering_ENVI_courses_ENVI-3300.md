### Course Description

This course examines the complex intersections between climate change and human society, with particular emphasis on ecological and socio-political tipping points—thresholds beyond which rapid, self-reinforcing, and potentially irreversible transformations occur. Students will explore the scientific foundations of climate systems alongside the societal, economic, and policy dimensions that both drive and respond to these transformations. Drawing on interdisciplinary frameworks, the course challenges students to critically assess the role of human agency in navigating an era of accelerating environmental change.

---

### Learning Objectives

Upon successful completion of this course, students will be able to:

- Define and distinguish between physical, ecological, and social tipping points, and evaluate their interdependencies within Earth-system models
- Analyze the disproportionate impacts of climate change on vulnerable populations and critically assess questions of environmental justice and equity
- Evaluate current and proposed climate policy frameworks at local, national, and international scales, identifying opportunities for and barriers to transformative action
- Apply systems-thinking methodologies to model feedback loops and cascading effects within coupled human-environment systems
- Construct evidence-based arguments about climate risk communication, public perception, and the role of institutional actors in shaping societal responses to climate change

### Assessment Methods

| Component | Weight |
|---|---|
| Participation & Weekly Response Posts | 10% |
| Midterm Examination (in-class, written) | 20% |
| Tipping Point Case Study Analysis (individual paper, ~2,500 words) | 25% |
| Policy Brief and Stakeholder Presentation (group project) | 25% |
| Final Examination (take-home, analytical essay format) | 20% |

**Case Study Analysis:** Students will select a specific tipping point scenario—such as Arctic sea ice loss, Amazon dieback, or the collapse of Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation—and produce a rigorous written analysis integrating scientific evidence with social and policy dimensions.

**Policy Brief and Stakeholder Presentation:** Working in groups of three to four, students will develop a professional policy brief addressed to a designated governmental or intergovernmental body, followed by a formal 15-minute presentation simulating a stakeholder advisory session.
