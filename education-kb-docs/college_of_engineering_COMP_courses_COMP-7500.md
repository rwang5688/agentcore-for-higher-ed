## Course Description

### Course Description

COMP-7500: Great Insights in Computing offers an advanced exploration of the landmark ideas, paradigms, and theoretical breakthroughs that have shaped the field of computer science from its foundational origins to contemporary frontiers. Students will engage critically with the intellectual history of computing, examining the conceptual contributions of seminal thinkers alongside the algorithmic, architectural, and systems-level innovations that continue to define the discipline. The course is designed to cultivate both historical perspective and analytical depth, equipping graduate students to situate current research challenges within the broader evolution of computational thought.

---

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

- Identify and critically analyze the foundational theoretical contributions in computer science, including computability, complexity theory, information theory, and algorithm design, evaluating their lasting significance to the discipline.
- Trace the intellectual lineage of major computing paradigms — including procedural, object-oriented, functional, and parallel computing — and assess how each paradigm emerged in response to practical and theoretical limitations of its predecessors.
- Synthesize primary and secondary scholarly literature to construct coherent, evidence-based arguments about the development and impact of specific computing innovations.
- Apply insights drawn from historical breakthroughs to contemporary research problems, demonstrating an ability to connect foundational principles with cutting-edge challenges in areas such as machine learning, distributed systems, and human-computer interaction.
- Communicate complex computational ideas clearly and rigorously through both written scholarship and oral presentation to an audience of peers and domain experts.

---

### Prerequisites

Students enrolling in COMP-7500 must have completed the following or demonstrate equivalent graduate-level preparation:

- **COMP-5100: Theory of Computation** or equivalent graduate-level exposure to formal languages, automata, and computability
- **COMP-5250: Algorithm Design and Analysis** or a course covering asymptotic analysis, NP-completeness, and algorithm paradigms
- **COMP-5410: Systems Architecture and Organization** or demonstrated familiarity with computer architecture fundamentals
- Graduate standing in Computer Science or a closely related field, or written permission of the supervising instructor

Students with undergraduate backgrounds in mathematics, electrical engineering, or cognitive science who can demonstrate proficiency in foundational computing theory may petition for prerequisite waiver through the Department of Computer Science graduate advisor.

### Assessment Methods

| Component | Weight | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Seminar Participation | 15% | Active, prepared engagement in weekly seminar discussions; students are expected to arrive having read all assigned materials and to contribute substantively to in-class dialogue. |
| Critical Reading Responses | 15% | Eight short written responses (400–600 words each) submitted prior to designated seminar sessions, demonstrating close reading and critical engagement with assigned primary texts. |
| Midterm Analytical Essay | 20% | A focused scholarly essay (2,500–3,000 words) analyzing a single landmark insight or theoretical development in computing, situating it within its intellectual and historical context. |
| Research Presentation | 15% | A 20-minute in-class presentation with Q&A, in which the student introduces and critically contextualizes a major computing breakthrough of their choosing, approved in advance by the instructor. |
| Final Research Paper | 35% | An original scholarly paper of 6,000–8,000 words examining the development, reception, and enduring significance of a substantial insight or cluster of related insights in computing history. The paper must engage with a minimum of 15 peer-reviewed sources and adhere to ACM or IEEE citation standards. |

Late submissions are subject to a penalty of 5% per calendar day unless an extension has been granted in writing by the primary section instructor prior to the due date.
