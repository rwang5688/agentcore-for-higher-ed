## Course Description

### Course Description

COMP-7000: Efficient Algorithms and Intractable Problems provides an advanced examination of algorithmic design paradigms, computational complexity theory, and the theoretical boundaries of efficient computation. Students will rigorously explore the classification of problems by tractability, investigating the foundational distinctions between polynomial-time solvable problems and those believed to be inherently intractable. This course bridges classical theoretical computer science with contemporary research challenges, equipping students with the analytical tools necessary to reason formally about algorithmic efficiency and problem hardness.

### Learning Objectives

Upon successful completion of this course, students will be able to:

- Design and analyze advanced algorithms using paradigms including dynamic programming, greedy methods, network flow, and approximation strategies, formally evaluating their time and space complexity
- Demonstrate a rigorous understanding of complexity classes, including P, NP, co-NP, PSPACE, and the polynomial hierarchy, and articulate the significance of unresolved questions such as the P vs. NP problem
- Construct formal proofs of NP-completeness and NP-hardness through polynomial-time reductions from established benchmark problems
- Evaluate the theoretical limits of computation by applying tools from randomized complexity, parameterized complexity, and inapproximability theory
- Synthesize theoretical findings to assess the practical implications of intractability, including the design of approximation algorithms and heuristic strategies for real-world computational problems

### Prerequisites

Students must have completed the following before enrolling in COMP-7000:

- **COMP-5200: Design and Analysis of Algorithms** or equivalent, with a grade of B or higher
- **COMP-5150: Theory of Computation**, covering formal languages, automata, and Turing machines
- **MATH-4100: Discrete Mathematics and Proof Techniques**, or demonstrated proficiency in formal mathematical reasoning
- Graduate standing in Computer Science or a closely related discipline, or instructor consent

Students without formal prerequisites who possess substantial professional or research experience in theoretical computer science may petition the department for a prerequisite waiver.

### Assessment Methods

| Component | Weight |
|---|---|
| Problem Sets (6 assignments) | 30% |
| Midterm Examination | 20% |
| Research Paper and Presentation | 25% |
| Final Examination | 20% |
| Participation and In-Class Exercises | 5% |

**Problem Sets:** Six rigorous written assignments distributed throughout the semester, requiring students to analyze algorithms, construct complexity proofs, and develop reductions. Collaboration is permitted in discussion, but written solutions must be composed independently.

**Midterm Examination:** A closed-book, in-person examination covering algorithm analysis, foundational complexity classes, and NP-completeness proof techniques administered at the midpoint of the semester.

**Research Paper and Presentation:** A formal written paper (approximately 4,000–6,000 words) investigating an approved topic at the frontier of complexity theory or algorithmic research, accompanied by a 20-minute in-class presentation with a question-and-answer period. Topic proposals must be submitted and approved by Week 4.

**Final Examination:** A comprehensive closed-book examination covering all course material, with emphasis on synthesis across topics and the construction of novel reductions and proofs.

**Participation:** Students are expected to engage meaningfully in lectures, seminars, and structured in-class problem-solving sessions.
