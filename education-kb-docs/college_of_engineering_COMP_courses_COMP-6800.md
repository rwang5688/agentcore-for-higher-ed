## Course Description

### Course Description

Discrete Structures provides an advanced treatment of the mathematical foundations essential to computer science, with emphasis on formal reasoning, combinatorial analysis, and the theoretical underpinnings of algorithms and computational systems. Students engage rigorously with topics including graph theory, logic, set theory, number theory, formal languages, and algebraic structures within the context of applied computing problems. The course bridges abstract mathematical theory with practical applications in algorithm design, cryptography, and software verification, preparing students for advanced graduate-level research and professional practice.

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

- Construct and evaluate formal mathematical proofs using techniques such as induction, contradiction, and direct proof as applied to discrete and combinatorial structures
- Analyze and apply graph-theoretic models to solve complex computational problems, including network flow, planarity, and graph coloring
- Demonstrate mastery of algebraic structures — including groups, rings, and lattices — and articulate their relevance to cryptographic systems and formal computation
- Apply combinatorial reasoning and generating functions to analyze the complexity and correctness of algorithms
- Formalize computational problems using logic and set-theoretic notation, and evaluate the expressive power of formal languages and automata

### Prerequisites

Students must have completed the following or equivalent coursework prior to enrollment:

- COMP-5200: Algorithm Analysis and Design (or equivalent graduate-level algorithms course)
- MATH-5100: Advanced Linear Algebra or MATH-5150: Abstract Algebra I
- COMP-4300: Theory of Computation or demonstrated proficiency in formal logic and mathematical proof techniques

Students who have not completed a proof-based mathematics course at the undergraduate or graduate level are strongly advised to consult the course instructor prior to registration. Admission to a graduate program in Computer Science or a related field is assumed.

### Assessment Methods

| Component | Weight |
|---|---|
| Problem Sets (6 assignments) | 30% |
| Midterm Examination | 20% |
| Research Paper and Presentation | 25% |
| Final Examination | 25% |

**Problem Sets:** Six written assignments distributed throughout the semester require students to construct formal proofs and solve computational problems drawn from each major topic area. Collaboration is permitted in discussion only; submitted work must be independently written.

**Midterm Examination:** A closed-book, in-person examination covering logic, set theory, number theory, and introductory graph theory. Students may bring one handwritten reference sheet.

**Research Paper and Presentation:** Students produce a 4,000–6,000 word scholarly paper applying discrete structures concepts to an original problem domain (e.g., network security, compiler optimization, distributed systems). A 20-minute presentation to the seminar group is required. A topic proposal must be submitted and approved by Week 4.

**Final Examination:** A comprehensive closed-book examination covering all course content, with particular emphasis on integration across topic areas and proof construction.
