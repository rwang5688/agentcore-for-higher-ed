### Course Description

This course provides students from non-computing disciplines with a broad and accessible foundation in the principles and practices of modern computing, including how computers process information, the basics of programming logic, and the role of technology in contemporary society. Students will gain practical experience with common software tools and develop an informed understanding of how computing systems shape and interact with their respective fields of study. No prior experience with computers beyond general everyday use is assumed or required.

---

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

- Explain fundamental concepts in computing, including hardware architecture, operating systems, networks, and data representation, in clear and accurate terms.
- Apply basic problem-solving and algorithmic thinking to construct simple logical workflows and pseudocode solutions to well-defined problems.
- Demonstrate practical proficiency with productivity software, file management systems, and introductory data tools commonly used in academic and professional environments.
- Evaluate the social, ethical, and security implications of computing technologies, including issues of privacy, digital citizenship, and the responsible use of information systems.
- Identify and describe ways in which computing intersects with and supports work in their own academic discipline or professional field of interest.

---

### Prerequisites

There are no formal course prerequisites for COMP-10100. Students are expected to have completed secondary-level education and to possess basic everyday familiarity with personal computing devices. This course is not open to students who have received credit for any Computer Science course at the 200 level or above, or to declared Computer Science majors.

### Assessment Methods

| Component | Weight |
|---|---|
| Participation & Weekly Reflections | 10% |
| Lab Assignments (6 throughout semester) | 25% |
| Midterm Examination | 20% |
| Technology & Society Short Essay (800–1,000 words) | 15% |
| Group Project: Computing in Your Field | 15% |
| Final Examination | 15% |

**Lab Assignments** are completed during scheduled lab sessions and cover hands-on skills including spreadsheet analysis, basic scripting tasks, and network tools. Students must attend the lab section in which they are enrolled.

**Technology & Society Short Essay** requires students to identify a current computing-related issue — such as algorithmic bias, data privacy, or digital accessibility — and analyze it critically using concepts introduced in the course.

**Group Project: Computing in Your Field** is a collaborative presentation in which student teams research and communicate how computing technologies are applied within a specific non-computing discipline of their choosing. Teams of three to four students will deliver a 12–15 minute in-class presentation accompanied by a written report of 1,500–2,000 words.

### Required Materials

- Haverstock, D. L., & Pinelli, C. R. (2021). *Computers, People, and Society: A Practical Introduction to Computing for the Modern World* (4th ed.). Greywood Academic Press.
- Albanese, T. F. (2020). *Thinking in Steps: An Introduction to Algorithms and Logic Without the Jargon* (2nd ed.). Crestfield University Press.
- Supplementary readings, lab guides, and tutorial resources will be made available at no additional cost through the Peculiar U course management portal. Students are advised to check the portal regularly, as materials are updated each semester to reflect current developments in computing and technology.

Students will require reliable access to a personal computer capable of running standard office productivity software. Specific software requirements — all of which are available free of charge through Peculiar U's institutional licensing agreements — will be communicated during the first week of class.
