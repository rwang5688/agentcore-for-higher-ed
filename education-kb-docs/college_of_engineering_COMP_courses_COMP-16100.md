## Course Description

### Course Description

COMP-16100: Responsible Computing examines the ethical, legal, societal, and professional dimensions of computing practice, with particular emphasis on the responsibilities of computing professionals operating in complex, high-stakes environments. Students will critically analyze issues including algorithmic bias, data privacy, surveillance, intellectual property, accessibility, and the environmental impact of computational systems. Drawing on frameworks from philosophy, law, and social science, this graduate-level course equips students to make principled decisions and advocate for ethical outcomes across diverse computing contexts.

---

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

- Evaluate the ethical implications of computing systems and technologies using established moral frameworks, including utilitarianism, deontological ethics, and virtue ethics, as applied to real-world computing scenarios
- Critically assess issues of algorithmic fairness, bias, and discrimination, and propose mitigation strategies appropriate to specific technical and organizational contexts
- Analyze the legal and regulatory landscape governing data privacy, intellectual property, cybersecurity, and digital rights across multiple jurisdictions
- Articulate professional responsibilities and accountability structures for computing practitioners, including obligations to employers, clients, the public, and the broader global community
- Design and evaluate responsible computing policies and governance frameworks suitable for deployment in organizational or institutional settings

### Assessment Methods

**Participation and Seminar Discussion (15%)**
Active, informed participation in weekly seminar discussions is expected. Students will be evaluated on the quality, depth, and intellectual rigor of their contributions, including engagement with assigned readings and peer arguments.

**Case Study Analysis Papers (25%)**
Students will complete three short analytical papers (approximately 1,500–2,000 words each) responding to structured case studies involving real or hypothetical ethical dilemmas in computing practice. Papers must demonstrate applied use of ethical frameworks and relevant legal or regulatory knowledge.

**Policy Brief (20%)**
Working individually, students will produce a graduate-level policy brief (2,500–3,500 words) directed at a specific audience — such as a government body, corporate board, or professional standards organization — recommending a course of action on a responsible computing issue of their choosing.

**Group Research Project and Presentation (25%)**
Teams of three to four students will conduct original research on a substantive responsible computing topic, producing a written report (8,000–10,000 words) and delivering a formal 25-minute presentation with a 10-minute Q&A to the seminar group and invited faculty reviewers.

**Final Examination (15%)**
A three-hour closed-book written examination testing students' understanding of core ethical frameworks, legal concepts, professional codes, and their ability to apply these to novel scenarios. The examination will include both short-answer and extended essay questions.
