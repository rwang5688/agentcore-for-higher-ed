This course provides a rigorous introduction to the mathematical foundations underlying the science of computation, examining the fundamental limits and capabilities of computational models. Students will explore formal languages, automata, computability, and complexity theory, developing the theoretical tools necessary to reason precisely about what computers can and cannot do. The course emphasises both formal proof techniques and the practical implications of theoretical results for the design and analysis of algorithms and computing systems.

### Learning Objectives

Upon successful completion of this course, students will be able to:

- Define and analyse formal language classes — including regular, context-free, and recursively enumerable languages — and apply the corresponding automata models to characterise each class.
- Construct and manipulate finite automata, pushdown automata, and Turing machines to model computational processes and demonstrate language acceptance or rejection.
- Apply formal proof techniques, including mathematical induction, diagonalisation, and reduction arguments, to establish results about computational problems.
- Articulate the Church–Turing thesis and explain the theoretical and philosophical significance of undecidability, with reference to canonical undecidable problems such as the Halting Problem.
- Classify computational problems within the complexity hierarchy, distinguishing between complexity classes such as P and NP, and reason about the implications of NP-completeness for practical problem solving.

### Prerequisites

Students enrolling in COMP-17500 must have completed the following:

- **COMP-10200 Introduction to Programming** or demonstrated equivalent programming experience
- **MATH-11400 Discrete Mathematics for Computing** (covering logic, sets, relations, functions, and proof techniques)
- **MATH-11100 Calculus I** or **MATH-11050 Foundations of Mathematical Reasoning**

Students are strongly advised to have a working familiarity with basic proof writing before attempting this course. Those who have not completed a formal discrete mathematics unit are encouraged to consult their academic advisor before enrolling.

### Assessment Methods

Student performance in COMP-17500 is evaluated through a combination of the following components:

| Assessment Component | Weight | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Problem Sets (×5) | 35% | Weekly written assignments requiring formal proofs, automata constructions, and problem classification exercises |
| Midterm Examination | 20% | In-class written examination covering automata theory and formal languages (Weeks 1–6) |
| Proof Portfolio | 15% | A curated collection of three to five original formal proofs demonstrating command of reduction and diagonalisation techniques |
| Final Examination | 30% | Comprehensive written examination covering all course material, with emphasis on complexity theory and undecidability |

Problem sets must be typeset using LaTeX and submitted electronically via the course management portal. Collaboration on problem sets is permitted in accordance with Peculiar U's Academic Integrity Policy; however, all submitted work must be written independently. The proof portfolio is an individual assessment and must represent the student's own original work entirely.
