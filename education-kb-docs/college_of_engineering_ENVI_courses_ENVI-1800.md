### Course Description

This advanced course provides a rigorous examination of the frameworks, treaties, and institutions governing environmental protection at the international level, with particular attention to the intersection of engineering practice, sovereign responsibility, and transboundary ecological harm. Students will critically analyse landmark conventions, customary international law, and emerging regulatory developments addressing issues such as climate change, biodiversity loss, marine pollution, and hazardous waste management. The course draws on comparative legal methodologies to equip students with the analytical tools necessary to navigate complex, multi-jurisdictional environmental challenges in professional and policy contexts.

---

### Learning Objectives

Upon successful completion of this course, students will be able to:

- Identify and interpret the principal sources of international environmental law, including multilateral treaties, customary norms, and soft law instruments, and articulate their legal weight and practical enforceability.
- Evaluate the roles and mandates of key international institutions — including the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP), the International Court of Justice, and treaty-specific bodies — in the development and enforcement of global environmental standards.
- Apply core legal principles such as the precautionary principle, the polluter-pays principle, common but differentiated responsibilities, and intergenerational equity to real-world engineering and environmental management scenarios.
- Critically assess the effectiveness and limitations of major international environmental agreements, including the Paris Agreement, the Convention on Biological Diversity, and the Basel Convention, in achieving their stated objectives.
- Construct well-reasoned legal arguments and policy recommendations concerning transboundary environmental disputes, integrating technical engineering knowledge with international legal analysis.

### Prerequisites

Students must have completed a minimum of 90 credit points at the undergraduate level and have prior coursework in at least two of the following areas: environmental science or environmental engineering fundamentals, public international law or legal studies, natural resource management, or policy analysis. Familiarity with basic principles of ecology and an introductory understanding of governmental or intergovernmental regulatory structures are strongly recommended. Students from disciplines outside law or environmental engineering are encouraged to consult with their assigned instructor prior to enrolment.

### Assessment Methods

**Participation and Weekly Response Papers (10%)**
Students are expected to engage actively in seminar discussions and submit brief weekly response papers (300–500 words) responding to assigned readings, demonstrating critical engagement with core concepts and emerging issues in international environmental law.

**Comparative Legal Analysis Assignment (20%)**
A written assignment of approximately 1,800–2,200 words requiring students to compare the legal frameworks of two international environmental agreements, evaluating their design, implementation mechanisms, and relative effectiveness. Submitted in Week 6.

**Moot Court / Negotiation Simulation (25%)**
Students will participate in a structured diplomatic or judicial simulation, representing assigned state or non-state parties in a hypothetical international environmental dispute. Assessment is based on preparation materials, oral performance, and a post-simulation reflective brief.

**Research Paper (30%)**
An original research paper of 3,500–4,500 words on a topic approved by the supervising instructor. Papers must demonstrate mastery of international legal sources, engage with engineering or technical dimensions of the chosen environmental issue, and propose actionable policy or regulatory recommendations.

**Final Examination (15%)**
A two-hour closed-book examination consisting of short-answer and essay questions designed to assess students' command of course content, their ability to apply legal principles to novel scenarios, and their understanding of the current state of international environmental governance.
